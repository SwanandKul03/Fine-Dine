The problem

Paper orders relied
on in-person updates.

Oblix is a premium fine-dining restaurant at The Shard. When I worked there, the low-lit evening setting made reading and recording orders more demanding. Waiters had no handheld order-taking device: we wrote orders on paper, then physically found the right chef, waiter or floor manager to pass on guest requirements, check preparation or share changes. Each update depended on reaching the right person while service continued.

Waiter

Waiters take orders, record guest requirements and coordinate service across multiple tables, keeping guests informed throughout.

Pain points

  • Low lighting makes handwritten orders difficult to record and read.
  • Paper notes make table, seat and guest requests harder to track.
  • Finding colleagues for updates takes time away from guests.
User goalsKeep orders accurate, give prompt answers and deliver attentive service—with less time spent chasing updates.
Chef

Chefs prepare dishes, track incoming orders and coordinate cooking times across the kitchen.

Pain points

  • Unclear orders or late changes disrupt preparation.
  • Guest requirements can get lost as orders build up.
  • Different cooking times make table orders difficult to coordinate.
User goalsDeliver accurate, well-timed dishes while maintaining quality during busy service.
Floor manager

Managers monitor service, support staff and handle guest concerns, keeping the floor and kitchen working together.

Pain points

  • Gathering updates across teams takes time.
  • Limited visibility makes delays harder to spot early.
  • Staff requests and guest concerns compete for attention.
User goalsResolve issues early, keep teams coordinated and deliver consistent service across the restaurant.

Read a moment from service+

When a guest asked about missing drinks, I first had to find who took the order and whether the bar had received a ticket. Sometimes that meant asking the guest to repeat the order. That moment showed me why each update needed to reach the right person without another trip between teams.

My design brief

I set out to help staff send accurate orders, see progress and agree who would collect, while staying available to guests.

Design decisions

Support the team.
Respect the guest’s time.

I followed service within a 90-minute dining frame. These decisions aim to reduce time spent chasing information, give staff a clearer next task and help guests receive attentive, informed service.

Preserve guest context

Carry the guest’s requirement through service.

Why this matters

On the floor, a guest’s allergy needed to stay clear even as orders and colleagues changed. I kept the requirement linked to the seat and visible during ordering, with a deliberate review before sending. The aim is to give the waiter a clear confirmation step and the kitchen the context it needs, so the guest does not have to repeat an important request.

Make preparation visible

Give staff an update they can act on.

Why this matters

During a timed visit, a waiter needs to explain a delayed course while still looking after other tables. I made preparation progress and the affected table visible so staff can plan their next task and give a relevant guest update. For chefs, separating preparation from collection is intended to reduce repeated status questions and protect time for preparing and plating.

Coordinate the handover

Coordinate the pickup without leaving gaps.

Why this matters

I saw how checking who would collect food could pull attention away from the dining room. I showed pass timing alongside a named collection owner so colleagues can recognise an assigned pickup and choose their next task. The intention is to help managers balance table checks and food delivery, while guests continue to have someone available on the floor.

Testing with colleagues

I tried the tasks we face during service.

I asked restaurant colleagues to try three familiar tasks in the prototype. Here is the problem, what they tried and the feedback they gave me.

Waiter

Send the order from the table.

The same order was written on paper, then entered again at the system.

Task I gave them

Choose the seat, check the guest requirement and send the order once.

Feedback I heard

Waiters valued less repeated entry and checking progress without leaving their section.

Floor manager

Find where the drinks order stopped.

A guest asked about missing drinks. I had to find the waiter and check whether the bar had a ticket.

Task I gave them

Check the table’s progress and decide who needs support before following up.

Feedback I heard

Managers valued seeing updates before stepping away from the stand to help.

Chef

Hand ready food to a named collector.

Finished dishes waited while chefs searched for somebody from the floor.

Task I gave them

Share readiness, then ask a floor colleague to claim the pickup.

Feedback I heard

Chefs valued a visible collector and fewer repeated calls interrupting preparation.

How these timings were recorded

The original order workflow took 3 minutes on paper and 7 minutes confirming and re-entering, from my service recollection. App-test figures were 1 minute for the menu, 3.91 for submission, 11.03 for drinks and 19 for starters. These are decimal minutes as supplied. Mains were reported at 37–40 minutes in both workflows. In the original workflow, first drinks arrived at 27 minutes and starters at 27–30 minutes. The second-drinks entry is “No order”. Guests were asked to sit on the other side for desserts in the final ten minutes, as the table had another booking. The original timing notes, sample size and common starting point are unavailable, so I have not calculated a percentage improvement. The drink and starter figures are arrival milestones, not preparation or pass-wait durations.

Outcomes & learnings

What colleagues valued
in the prototype.

Their feedback gave me a clear direction: keep service information close to the task, so staff can give more attention to guests.

Waiters

Stay available to guests.

Record the order once and check progress without leaving the section.

What I heard

Waiters valued less reliance on memory and repeated entry, with more confidence when answering guests or passing on changes.

Managers

See where support is needed.

Check the shared status before interrupting a colleague or leaving the stand.

What I heard

Managers valued an overview of open work so they could support the right table or colleague while organising the rest of the shift.

Chefs

Focus on preparing the food.

Share readiness and see who is collecting, with fewer repeated calls to the floor.

What I heard

Chefs valued clearer requirements and collection ownership, giving them more attention for preparation, plating and handing work over.

My main learning

Mains stayed at 37–40 minutes because guests set the pace of their meal. My design needs to make updates, repeat orders and table handovers clearer, while giving guests the time to enjoy each course.

What I would test in live service

Compare both workflows across a 90-minute visit, using the same start and finish for each task. Measure order submission, collection waits and repeated checks alongside guest contact and staff workload.

Project background

Why I started
this project.

I initiated this independent project while working in operations at Oblix. Coordinating sections and responding to service issues helped me identify the people, information and handovers the product needed to support.

I took on this independent project alongside my operational role. It gave me a practical way to apply UX methods to service problems I saw first-hand, and to consider how a digital interface changes the work around it.
