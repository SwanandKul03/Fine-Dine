01 / The problem

Service updates depended
on finding a colleague.

At Oblix, a waiter could take an order at the table but still need to leave the section to check preparation or pass on a request. I explored how a shared team app could make those handovers clearer in a busy, low-lit dining room.

02 / How I approached it

Observe the service.

Design decisions

Three handovers.
Three interface decisions.

01 / Preserve guest context

Keep the allergy linked to the seat.

The updated ordering screen keeps the allergy requirement visible above the seat and menu controls. Send opens an allergy check with Back and Confirm & Send, keeping the requirement visible at submission.

02 / Make preparation visible

Show preparation before collection.

Kitchen summarises readiness, highlights a delay and identifies the affected table. A preparation update and a collection claim represent different responsibilities.

03 / Coordinate the handover

Make collection ownership visible.

Alerts brings safety information and time-sensitive pass items into one view. A named collection state helps colleagues recognise work that is already assigned.

05 / Evaluation

What I reviewed.
What still needs testing.

I refined the prototype after informal testing with restaurant colleagues. The supplied export demonstrates the current design. Without participant-level records or measured results, I separate the visible refinements from the next evaluation tasks.

Testing evidence and project scope +

The prototype was supplied as a version refined after testing. The available export does not include participant details, task records or measured service outcomes. The design notes describe the supplied interface, rather than unrecorded usability findings.

This is an independent product concept informed by operational experience. Restaurant deployment, POS integration, permissions and synchronisation would need validation with the teams. All current app screens come from the latest supplied export. The service model describes proposed responsibilities and dependencies.

Outcomes & learnings

What the concept delivers.
What remains unresolved.

Product outcome

A working team-app concept.

The prototype presents floor priorities, seat requirements, kitchen progress and collection ownership across three core areas.

Service-design outcome

A proposed handover model.

The proposed map connects guest requests with floor actions, kitchen preparation and collection. Team agreement on ownership and escalation is still needed.

Key learning

Each status needs an owner.

A status label helps only when staff can interpret it and understand their next step under service pressure.

Next: evaluate under service conditions.

Test one-handed use and status comprehension in low light, including missed updates and mistaken collection claims. Agree who maintains each status, how delays escalate and what staff do when the app is unavailable. Measure repeated checks and handover errors before claiming service improvements.

Project background

Why I started
this project.

I initiated this independent project while working in operations at Oblix. Coordinating sections and responding to service issues helped me identify the people, information and handovers the product needed to support.

I took on this independent project alongside my operational role. It gave me a practical way to apply UX methods to service problems I saw first-hand, and to consider how a digital interface changes the work around it.
